01

Before you sign

You asked how to get ahead of the contract. This is the answer: the contract itself is the highest-leverage thing on your desk this week. Nothing in the next twenty-nine days moves the number as much as getting these twelve answers in writing while you still have negotiating room.

The commission math does not work as stated — and you need to know which way

You were told 20% on products. In the IT channel, a reseller weighted toward hardware runs roughly a 6% profit margin overall, and gross margin on brand-name hardware is commonly single digits to low teens. If "20%" meant 20% of the sell price, PCC-IT would lose money on every hardware order you wrote. So it almost certainly means 20% of gross profit — which is a normal, even respectable, channel rate. But look at what the ambiguity is worth:

A $500,000 hardware orderGross profit @10%You are paid
If 20% is calculated on gross profit$50,000$10,000
If 20% is calculated on revenue$50,000$100,000

Same deal. Ten times the paycheck. Do not build a forecast, quit an option, or turn down other work until this sentence exists in your contract: "Commission is calculated on ____, defined as ____." Ask them to work one real past deal end-to-end on paper and attach it as an exhibit.

The twelve questions

Send these as one numbered email before you sign. A good partner answers all twelve without friction. How they respond tells you as much as what they say.

  1. Is commission calculated on gross revenue or gross profit — and what is the formula?
    Get a worked example attached to the agreement. Ask separately for both the product rate and the services rate, because they may use different bases.
  2. What counts as a "PCC-IT product" versus resold hardware?
    There may be a real distinction — their own solution bundles, licensing, and subscriptions could genuinely carry 20% of revenue while pass-through hardware carries 20% of margin. If so, that tells you exactly what to sell.
  3. When is commission earned, and when is it paid?
    On booking, on invoice, or on customer payment? Government agencies pay in 60–120 days. A deal you close in November could pay in March. This single answer determines whether you can stop driving.
  4. What happens to recurring service commission if either party terminates?
    This is the difference between building an annuity and renting one. Most 1099 agreements kill residuals at termination. Ask for post-termination residuals — even a reduced rate or a fixed tail of 24–36 months. Ask now; you will never have more leverage than before signing.
  5. What is the house-account and excluded-account list?
    Get it in writing before you prospect. Otherwise you will spend two weeks building a target list and discover half of it was never yours to sell.
  6. What is the deal registration process, and how is territory protected?
    Once you register an account, is it yours? For how long? What happens if another PCC-IT rep — or another TD SYNNEX reseller — has already registered it upstream?
  7. What is the quote turnaround SLA, and who do I send requests to?
    You will lose deals you earned if a quote takes a week. Get a named person and a committed turnaround. Test it in week one with a real request.
  8. Which contract vehicles are currently active, with expiration dates?
    Their capabilities page literally reads "Update in Progress." Quoting a dead vehicle to a contracting officer ends your credibility with that agency permanently. See the arsenal section for the specific ones to verify.
  9. Is there a SEWP VI letter of authorization coming from TD SYNNEX?
    SEWP V was extended only through 30 September 2026 — fifteen days from now. SEWP VI was awarded in June and goes live 1 November 2026. If PCC-IT does not have a SEWP VI LOA, that federal door is shut, and your federal plan has to route through GSA, CIO-CS or ITES-SW2 instead.
  10. How is Wide Open Sky AI treated — and can it subcontract?
    You own an AI services company; they sell AI services. Get a written carve-out so your own business is never a conflict claim. Then ask the better question: can WOSA be a named subcontractor on PCC-IT engagements? That would turn a conflict into a second revenue line.
  11. What do I get to represent you with?
    An @itpccit.com address, business cards, a LinkedIn title, the current line card, case studies with permission to name the customer, and three referenceable clients. A rep with a gmail address does not get a meeting with a state CIO.
  12. Who on the AI and engineering bench can join my calls?
    They claim 500+ AI specialists and 200+ data, ERP, network and security specialists. Ask to meet one this month. If that bench is real, it is your biggest asset. If it is a partner network on paper, you need to know before you promise it to a customer.

Also settle, in the same email

  • Expenses: approval threshold, reimbursement timeline, whether you front travel
  • E&O and general liability: are you covered under their policy, or do you need your own as a 1099?
  • CRM: theirs or yours? If theirs, get access day one. If yours, your HubSpot works fine.
  • Tools: ask them to fund LinkedIn Sales Navigator and a public-sector data seat (GovSpend or Deltek GovWin). It is cheaper than your ramp.
  • Intro: a working session with whoever runs their TD SYNNEX public-sector desk
  • Taxes: 1099 means self-employment tax on top of income tax. Set aside ~30% quarterly from dollar one.

03

The five lanes

Run all five in parallel, but weight your hours the way the lanes actually pay. Lanes A through C close in weeks to a few months. Lanes D and E close in quarters. A rep with no base who spends thirty days only on Fortune 500 has a beautiful pipeline and an empty bank account in March.

Lane A · California state agencies

CYCLE 30–90 DAYS  ·  HIGHEST PRIORITY

California's SB/DVBE Option lets any state department award a contract from $5,000.01 to $249,999.99 — for goods, services, or IT — to a certified small business with two quotes and no advertising. PCC-IT is a DGS-certified small business. Departments have participation goals they are measured on and actively need vendors to hit them.

This is the single shortest legal path from cold to closed that exists in your territory, and almost no VAR rep works it properly. Your entry point is not the CIO — it is the department's Small Business Advocate, a published, named person whose actual job is to take your meeting.

Lane B · K-12 districts via PEPPM

CYCLE 3–10 WEEKS  ·  HIGHEST VOLUME

Thirty-nine PEPPM contracts, piggybackable nationally, means a district can buy from PCC-IT without running its own bid. That removes the single biggest obstacle in public sector selling. 06

The AI wedge

You asked how to use Wide Open Sky AI to sell PCC-IT's AI offering. The honest answer is better than the obvious one: your AI background is not primarily how you sell AI. It is how you get meetings that end in infrastructure orders.

Why it works

Every VAR rep in the country is now saying the word "AI." Almost none of them have deployed anything. You have: a live conversational avatar in production for a real client, a HubSpot-integrated lead pipeline, workflow automation, performance dashboards, and a knowledge base you wrote yourself.

That means you can say something no catalog rep can say, and have it be true: "I've built and shipped this. It's never the model that breaks — it's the data, the network, and the security review." That one sentence separates you from everyone else who called that CIO this month.

Why it pays

Nobody buys a $2M AI program from a rep they met last week. But an honest AI conversation surfaces, every single time: the data isn't clean, the network can't carry it, security won't approve it, and there isn't enough compute.

Those four findings map directly to data engineering, network refresh, zero-trust and penetration testing, and NVIDIA/Dell/HPE/Lenovo hardware — all of it on PCC-IT's line card, all of it on a contract vehicle, all of it closeable in a fraction of the time. Lead with AI. Close on infrastructure.

07

Pitfalls

Sorted by what they cost you. Deal-enders can end the relationship or the income. Expensive costs you a quarter. Friction costs you deals you had already earned.

Deal-ender

Signing before the commission base is defined

Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the only mistake here you cannot undo. A tenfold ambiguity in writing is not a detail.

Deal-ender

Recurring commission that dies on termination

The 15% recurring is the entire long-term case for this job. If it stops the day you stop, you are not building an asset — you are renting one, and you will have no leverage later. Negotiate a residual tail now.

Deal-ender

An unresolved conflict with Wide Open Sky AI

You own a company that sells what your principal sells. Undisclosed, that is a claim waiting to be made against your own business. Disclosed and carved out, it is an advantage.

Deal-ender

Running out of cash before month five

No draw, no base, 1099, and a six-to-nine-month enterprise cycle. First real check lands month three to five, and that assumes commission is paid on invoice rather than on collection. Protect the Uber and WOSA income until a PCC-IT check has actually cleared.

Expensive

Quoting a dead contract vehicle

Their own capabilities page says "Update in Progress." Cite an expired SEWP or DIR contract to a contracting officer and that agency is closed to you permanently. Verify every vehicle before it appears in an email.

Expensive

Selling 1.2 million products

The catalog is the trap. A rep who can sell anything sells nothing, because no buyer can tell what you are for. Pick three offers and repeat them for ninety days: the AI readiness assessment, a security assessment leading to managed security, and a network refresh on a co-op contract.

Expensive

Spending thirty days on cold Fortune 500

Where PCC-IT is least differentiated and the incumbents are most entrenched. Go there through a warm human or a supplier diversity office, never through a cold email to a CIO who already has an SHI account team on site.

Expensive

Chasing RFPs you did not help shape

A public RFP you learn about from a portal is usually already wired to an incumbent who helped write the requirements. Either shape the spec six months out or use the no-bid lanes. Bidding blind burns days and teaches you nothing.

Expensive

Burning your warm network with one generic email

Twenty years of relationships can be spent in a single afternoon. One at a time, personal, no ask in the first message. These contacts are worth more than the entire rest of the target list combined.

Expensive

Measuring activity instead of meetings

Five hundred emails sent and zero meetings booked is a failure that feels like work. The only week-one metrics that matter are meetings held and opportunities created.

Friction

A slow quote desk

Test it in week one with a real request. If the turnaround is longer than 48 hours, you need to know now and build your own workaround rather than discovering it mid-deal.

Friction

Channel conflict on deal registration

The same end customer may already be registered to another TD SYNNEX reseller. Register early, register everything, and confirm in writing.

Friction

Government procurement rules you have not had to know before

Many agencies prohibit accepting so much as a lunch. Contacting the wrong person during an active solicitation can disqualify the bid entirely. During any open procurement, talk only to the contracting officer.

Friction

Cold email to .gov landing in quarantine

A brand-new sending domain blasting government addresses gets filtered. Warm it: 10–15 personalized sends a day for the first week or two, then scale.

Friction

Single-threading

One champion who takes another job takes the deal with them. Three contacts per live opportunity, minimum — and in public sector, one of them should always be on the procurement side.

Friction

Forgetting the self-employment tax

1099 income carries roughly 15.3% self-employment tax before income tax. A $150,000 1099 year is meaningfully less in hand than a $150,000 salary. Set aside ~30% quarterly from the first check.

On your $150K target

You said you would be satisfied at $150,000 in year one with a flexible schedule. On the default basis, that sits above the base case and short of the strong case — possible, but not the way to bet. The realistic band is $60,000–$90,000 in year one, with $150,000 becoming likely in year two if you have built recurring services by then.

The lever that changes everything

Recurring services commission is the only part of this job that compounds. A single $10,000/month managed services contract pays you roughly $18,000 a year without being re-sold. Five of them is $90,000 a year of income you do not have to go get again.

Hardware closes faster and feels more productive. Services build the asset. Weight your hours roughly 60/40 toward services, even in month one when hardware is the easier yes — and make sure the residual survives termination before you build a book you do not own.

About "up to $500,000"

On the default basis, $500,000 in commission requires roughly $8–10 million in annual product revenue plus more than $150,000 in monthly recurring services. That is a book of business built over many years, usually inherited. It is a real number for a real person at that firm — it is not a year-one number, and no plan on earth makes it one. Treat it as the ceiling of the role, not the forecast.

Discovery call framework — 25 minutes
STRUCTURE, NOT A SCRIPT  ·  TALK LESS THAN HALF THE TIME
0–2   FRAME
      "I've got 25 minutes on the calendar. I'd like to spend most of
      it understanding what you're dealing with, and I'll tell you
      honestly whether we're useful. Fair?"

2–10  SITUATION — let them talk
      What's on your roadmap for the next two or three quarters?
      What's driving that — a mandate, a refresh cycle, an incident,
      a budget window?
      How do you buy today? Who do you buy through?
      What's the part of your environment you'd fix first if the
      money appeared tomorrow?

10–18 CONSEQUENCE — this is where deals are made
      What happens if that doesn't get done this year?
      Who else feels it when it goes wrong?
      Has anyone tried to fix it before? What stopped it?

18–22 FIT — be willing to say no
      "Two of those we're strong on. The third I'd send you elsewhere
      for." (Say this when it's true. It buys you everything later.)

22–25 PATH
      "If this turns out to be right, what does it take to get a PO
      issued — and by when?"
      Who else needs to be in the next conversation?
      Agree the specific next step and date before you hang up.

ALWAYS  Log it in HubSpot the same hour. Send the recap email the
        same day, with the next step and date in the first line.
Why it works: the consequence block is what separates a pleasant call from a qualified opportunity — situation questions tell you what they want, consequence questions tell you whether they will actually spend. Naming one thing you are not right for is the fastest way to be believed about the things you are. And the PO question at minute 22 surfaces the procurement path while it is still cheap to learn.
