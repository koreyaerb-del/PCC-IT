01

The contract

The agreement dated 16 September answers the question this plan opened with, and the answer is below both options we modeled. Commission is paid on "sales net profit" — not revenue, not gross profit. Everything else in this section follows from that one definition, plus one deadline that is already running.

What "sales net profit" actually means

Schedule I defines it as "the remaining purchase-specific revenue after cost of goods sold, travel expenses, finance costs, labor costs, and all other expenses specifically related to that sale are subtracted."

That is a net-margin definition, not a gross-margin one. Your commission is calculated after PCC-IT deducts its cost of goods and its financing cost on the order and internal labor and an open-ended category called "all other expenses." Two consequences:

A $500,000 hardware order at 10% gross marginBasisYou are paid
20% of revenue$500,000$100,000
20% of gross profit$50,000$10,000
20% of "sales net profit" — as written~$35,000~$7,000

Run it against their own goal. Schedule I sets a $2,000,000 gross revenue target. At a typical 10% gross margin reduced to roughly 7% after the listed deductions, $2M of product revenue produces about $140,000 of "sales net profit" — and 20% of that is roughly $28,000. Hitting the number written into your own contract pays about twenty-eight thousand dollars.

The second problem is who holds the pen. "All other expenses specifically related to that sale" is undefined, uncapped, and calculated entirely by PCC-IT — and the agreement gives you no commission statement and no audit right. You would have no way to verify your own pay. The single highest-value change you can ask for is replacing "sales net profit" with "gross profit, meaning revenue less cost of goods sold." Depending on how heavy those deductions run, that one substitution is worth roughly 40–60% more pay — and, more importantly, it turns your commission into something you can verify.

The deadline already running: your Pre-Existing Client List

Section 6 says you keep full ownership of every contact, lead and client that existed as of the effective date — but only if you deliver that list to PCC-IT within 30 days. Effective date is 16 September, so the list is due on or about 16 October 2026. Miss it and the carve-out evaporates.

Build it wide and build it this week: every Wide Open Sky AI client and prospect, every Axcept Media and LearnYour Benefits contact, everything currently in your HubSpot, and every company on the target lists in this plan that you have already touched. Send it by email with a date stamp and keep the sent copy. Then ask for one fix: as drafted, the carve-out protects you from Section 6 only — Section 5's exclusivity and its damages clause are not carved out. Ask for the Pre-Existing Client List to be excluded from Section 5 as well, and for Wide Open Sky AI to be named in the agreement by name.

An honest overall read

This is a company-favorable agreement, which is normal for a commission-only contractor role. Most of it is ordinary. The indemnity is fair, the territory is broad, the schedule flexibility is real and in writing, and Section 6's carve-out shows they were willing to protect your existing network.

Four things are not ordinary: the net-profit definition with no audit right, forfeiting unpaid commission on termination, the perpetual restriction backed by 6x damages, and an IP assignment that isn't limited to work you do for them. Those four are worth negotiating over, and how they respond is itself the most useful information you'll get. A partner who fixes the definition and the termination forfeiture is one worth building a book with. A partner who won't discuss either has told you what the next two years look like.

This is a business read, not legal advice. Have your attorney review Sections 5, 9 and 14 and the Wyoming choice of law before signing.

The five lanes

Run all five in parallel, but weight your hours the way the lanes actually pay. Lanes A through C close in weeks to a few months. Lanes D and E close in quarters. A rep with no base who spends thirty days only on Fortune 500 has a beautiful pipeline and an empty bank account in March.

Lane A · California state agencies

CYCLE 30–90 DAYS  ·  HIGHEST PRIORITY

California's SB/DVBE Option lets any state department award a contract from $5,000.01 to $249,999.99 — for goods, services, or IT — to a certified small business with two quotes and no advertising. PCC-IT is a DGS-certified small business. Departments have participation goals they are measured on and actively need vendors to hit them.

This is the single shortest legal path from cold to closed that exists in your territory, and almost no VAR rep works it properly. Your entry point is not the CIO — it is the department's Small Business Advocate, a published, named person whose actual job is to take your meeting.

Lane B · K-12 districts via PEPPM

CYCLE 3–10 WEEKS  ·  HIGHEST VOLUME

Thirty-nine PEPPM contracts, piggybackable nationally, means a district can buy from PCC-IT without running its own bid. That removes the single biggest obstacle in public sector selling. Districts buy switches, access points, firewalls, Chromebooks, displays and licensing on a predictable annual cycle.

The unfair advantage nobody uses: E-Rate Form 470 filings are public data. Every district that wants to buy network equipment with federal subsidy must post what it needs and who to contact. You can read the entire national demand pipeline for free at opendata.usac.org and call the named person on the form. Ask whether PCC-IT holds an E-Rate SPIN.

Why it works

Every VAR rep in the country is now saying the word "AI." Almost none of them have deployed anything. You have: a live conversational avatar in production for a real client, a HubSpot-integrated lead pipeline, workflow automation, performance dashboards, and a knowledge base you wrote yourself.

That means you can say something no catalog rep can say, and have it be true: "I've built and shipped this. It's never the model that breaks — it's the data, the network, and the security review." That one sentence separates you from everyone else who called that CIO this month.

Nobody buys a $2M AI program from a rep they met last week. But an honest AI conversation surfaces, every single time: the data isn't clean, the network can't carry it, security won't approve it, and there isn't enough compute.

Those four findings map directly to data engineering, network refresh, zero-trust and penetration testing, and NVIDIA/Dell/HPE/Lenovo hardware — all of it on PCC-IT's line card, all of it on a contract vehicle, all of it closeable in a fraction of the time. Lead with AI. Close on infrastructure.

Pitfalls

Sorted by what they cost you. Deal-enders can end the relationship or the income. Expensive costs you a quarter. Friction costs you deals you had already earned.

Deal-ender

Signing the "sales net profit" definition unchanged

Confirmed in Schedule I, and worse than either case we modeled. It is uncapped, undefined at the edges, calculated solely by PCC-IT, and you have no audit right. Hitting their own $2M goal pays about $28,000.

Deal-ender

Forfeiting unpaid commission on termination

Section 2 earns commission only on the client's full payment, and rights end at termination. Close a $1M deal in January, get terminated in February, get paid nothing in March. PCC-IT can terminate without cause effective the day of notice. This is the largest single financial risk in the document.

Deal-ender

Missing the 16 October Pre-Existing Client List deadline

Thirty days from the effective date to claim your own network, or the Section 6 carve-out is gone permanently. This is the one item on this page with a hard date.

Deal-ender

No carve-out for Wide Open Sky AI

Section 5 bars similar business with anyone you have had a two-way exchange with, "during the term and thereafter," backed by six times the lost gross profit in damages. Section 9 assigns new developments without limiting them to PCC-IT work. Neither clause mentions WOSA, and neither has a time limit.

Deal-ender

Running out of cash before month five

No draw, no base, and commission now tied to customer payment rather than booking. First real money is month four or five at the earliest. Protect the Uber and WOSA income until a PCC-IT check has actually cleared.

Expensive

Quoting a dead contract vehicle

Their own capabilities page says "Update in Progress." Cite an expired SEWP or DIR contract to a contracting officer and that agency is closed to you permanently. Verify every vehicle before it appears in an email.

Expensive

Selling 1.2 million products

The catalog is the trap. A rep who can sell anything sells nothing, because no buyer can tell what you are for. Pick three offers and repeat them for ninety days: the AI readiness assessment, a security assessment leading to managed security, and a network refresh on a co-op contract.

Expensive

Spending thirty days on cold Fortune 500

Where PCC-IT is least differentiated and the incumbents are most entrenched. Go there through a warm human or a supplier diversity office, never through a cold email to a CIO who already has an SHI account team on site.

Expensive

Chasing RFPs you did not help shape

A public RFP you learn about from a portal is usually already wired to an incumbent who helped write the requirements. Either shape the spec six months out or use the no-bid lanes. Bidding blind burns days and teaches you nothing.

Expensive

Burning your warm network with one generic email

Twenty years of relationships can be spent in a single afternoon. One at a time, personal, no ask in the first message. These contacts are worth more than the entire rest of the target list combined.

Expensive

Measuring activity instead of meetings

Five hundred emails sent and zero meetings booked is a failure that feels like work. The only week-one metrics that matter are meetings held and opportunities created.

Friction

A slow quote desk

Test it in week one with a real request. If the turnaround is longer than 48 hours, you need to know now and build your own workaround rather than discovering it mid-deal.

Friction

Channel conflict on deal registration

The same end customer may already be registered to another TD SYNNEX reseller. Register early, register everything, and confirm in writing.

Friction

Government procurement rules you have not had to know before

Many agencies prohibit accepting so much as a lunch. Contacting the wrong person during an active solicitation can disqualify the bid entirely. During any open procurement, talk only to the contracting officer.

Friction

Cold email to .gov landing in quarantine

A brand-new sending domain blasting government addresses gets filtered. Warm it: 10–15 personalized sends a day for the first week or two, then scale.

Friction

Single-threading

One champion who takes another job takes the deal with them. Three contacts per live opportunity, minimum — and in public sector, one of them should always be on the procurement side.

Friction

Forgetting the self-employment tax

1099 income carries roughly 15.3% self-employment tax before income tax. A $150,000 1099 year is meaningfully less in hand than a $150,000 salary. Set aside ~30% quarterly from the first check.

On your $150K target

You said you would be satisfied at $150,000 in year one with a flexible schedule. Under the agreement as written, that is not reachable in year one and probably not in year two. Win the gross-profit change and the picture shifts materially: a strong year clears $150,000 and a base year roughly $47,000, with recurring services compounding underneath. The negotiation is worth more than any thirty days of selling you could do. Have it before you sign.

The lever that changes everything

Recurring services commission is the only part of this job that compounds. A single $10,000/month managed services contract pays you roughly $18,000 a year without being re-sold. Five of them is $90,000 a year of income you do not have to go get again.

But not under this agreement. Section 2 ends Managed Services commission at the close of the month in which the agreement terminates — and PCC-IT can terminate without cause, effective the day of notice. As drafted you cannot build an annuity here; you can only rent one, and they hold the lease. Win the residual tail in question 8 and the 60/40 weighting toward services is right. Lose it, and your time is better spent on transactional product volume you get paid for immediately. That single answer should change how you spend the next year.

About "up to $500,000"

Under the signed definition, 20% of net profit works out to roughly 1.4% of product sell price. Reaching $500,000 in commission would take something on the order of $35 million in annual product revenue. Even on a gross-profit basis it needs $8–10 million plus a large services book. The figure quoted in your interview is not achievable under the compensation language in this agreement by anyone, in any year — which is itself a fair and non-confrontational thing to raise: "Help me understand how someone reaches that number under this definition." Their answer will tell you whether the definition is really what they meant.
